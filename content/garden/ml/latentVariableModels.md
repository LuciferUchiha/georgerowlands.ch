Most of the data we care about is high-dimensional, but its meaning is not. A $28 \times 28$ image of a handwritten digit lives in $\mathbb{R}^{784}$, yet what actually varies from one image to the next is a handful of factors: which digit it is, how it is rotated, the stroke width, the writing style. The pixels are a redundant, high-dimensional rendering of a compact, low-dimensional cause. **Latent variable models** make this idea precise. They posit that each observation $\mathbf{x}$ is generated from an unobserved **latent variable** $\mathbf{z}$ that carries the semantically meaningful factors of variation, and they give us a principled way to learn both the generating mechanism and the hidden $\mathbf{z}$ from data alone.

This is the same instinct behind [PCA](/garden/maths/linearAlgebra/pca) and [autoencoders](/garden/ml/computerVision/autoencoders), which also seek a low-dimensional representation that explains high-dimensional data. The difference is that here the representation is explicitly **probabilistic**: we write down a joint distribution $p(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z})$ over observations and latents, and everything else follows from the rules of probability. This note develops the framework through its most important instance, the **mixture model**, derives the **Expectation-Maximization (EM)** algorithm that fits it, and specializes the result to the **Gaussian mixture model**. The topic and matrix-factorization models built on the same foundation are developed in the [topic models](/garden/ml/topicModels) note.

## The Data Generating Process

A latent variable model assumes that an observation $\mathbf{x}$ is produced in two steps.

1. **Sample a latent variable** $\mathbf{z} \sim p(\mathbf{z})$. This $\mathbf{z}$ encodes the semantically meaningful factors of variation, for example the content and pose of the objects in an image. It is dense, low-dimensional information.
2. **Render an observation** $\mathbf{x} \sim p(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{z})$. The high-dimensional $\mathbf{x}$ is a noisy, redundant realization of the compact $\mathbf{z}$.

The hidden variables are everywhere once you look for them. A patient's observed symptoms are driven by an underlying disease, test scores reflect an unobserved trait like intelligence, gene expression profiles are shaped by hidden cell types, the words in a document are generated by its topics, and purchase patterns reflect latent customer segments. In every case we observe something high-dimensional and complicated, and we believe a simpler hidden structure explains it.

Whatever the latent variable means, the observed data is described by the **marginal** distribution $p(\mathbf{x})$, obtained by summing the joint over all possible values of the latent. For a continuous latent this is an integral, and for a discrete latent it is a sum:

$$
p(\mathbf{x}) = \int p(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z}) \, d\mathbf{z} = \int p(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{z}) \, p(\mathbf{z}) \, d\mathbf{z}
$$

This factorization into a **prior** $p(\mathbf{z})$ and a **class-conditional** (or likelihood) $p(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{z})$ is the defining structure. We specify a simple **complete data model** $p(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z})$, which is easy to write down because it follows the generative story, and then derive the marginal model $p(\mathbf{x})$ that we can actually compare against data. The art is that the marginal can be rich and multimodal even when each piece of the joint is simple.

{{< callout type="warning" title="Latent variables are not free lunch" >}}
A latent variable model is a modelling hope, not a guarantee. Four caveats are worth keeping in mind.

- **Identifiability:** different parameter settings can yield the exact same marginal $p(\mathbf{x})$. Permuting the labels of the hidden classes, for instance, leaves $p(\mathbf{x})$ unchanged, so there is no single "true" set of latents to recover.
- **No guaranteed interpretability:** the latents capture statistical structure, which need not line up with human-understandable concepts. Semantic meaning is something we hope for, not something the model is forced to deliver.
- **Entanglement:** a single coordinate $z_j$ may mix several factors of variation at once, for example encoding both an object's shape and its colour. Pulling these apart is the goal of **disentanglement**, an active research area.
- **Model misspecification:** the true data generating process may not lie in our assumed model family at all.
{{< /callout >}}

## Mixture Models

The simplest latent variable model takes the latent to be a single **categorical** variable: a label that says which of $k$ groups a data point belongs to. This is a **mixture model**, and it is the probabilistic backbone of clustering.

We have a data set of patterns $\{\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d : i = 1, \dots, n\}$. With each pattern we associate a latent class variable $Z_i \in \{1, \dots, k\}$ that tags it as a member of one of $k$ groups. The latent follows a categorical law, where the prior probabilities $\boldsymbol{\pi} = (\pi_1, \dots, \pi_k)$ are the **mixing proportions**:

$$
Z_i \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Categorical}(\boldsymbol{\pi}), \qquad P(Z_i = z) = \pi_z, \qquad \pi_z \ge 0, \quad \sum_{z=1}^k \pi_z = 1
$$

We typically do not know in advance what the classes mean. The hope is to discover meaningful groups through clustering. Each class $z$ has its own **class-conditional distribution** $p(\mathbf{x} \mid z) = p(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta}_z)$ with its own parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}_z$, so the complete data model is

$$
p(\mathbf{x}, z) = \pi_z \, p(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta}_z)
$$

Marginalizing over the hidden class recovers the observed-data distribution as a **convex combination** of the class-conditionals, weighted by the mixing proportions:

$$
p(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \sum_{z=1}^k p(\mathbf{x}, z) = \sum_{z=1}^k \pi_z \, p(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta}_z), \qquad \boldsymbol{\theta} = (\boldsymbol{\pi}, \boldsymbol{\theta}_1, \dots, \boldsymbol{\theta}_k)
$$

Because $\pi_z \ge 0$ and $\sum_z \pi_z = 1$, this is literally a weighted average of distributions, which is why a mixture of simple unimodal pieces can represent a complicated multimodal whole. The mixing proportions answer "how common is each group", for instance how prevalent a disease is in the population, while the class-conditionals answer "what does a member of this group look like".

### Probabilistic Clustering

Mixture models connect to clustering through the **posterior** over the latent class. Having observed a point $\mathbf{x}$, Bayes' rule tells us how to distribute belief over which class generated it:

$$
P(Z = z \mid \mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{\pi_z \, p(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta}_z)}{\sum_{\zeta=1}^k \pi_\zeta \, p(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta}_\zeta)}
$$

Read the pieces from the structure of the fraction. The numerator $\pi_z \, p(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta}_z)$ is the joint probability that the point both belongs to class $z$ (how likely class $z$ is in general) and looks like $\mathbf{x}$ (how well class $z$ explains this point). If it is large, the point was very likely sampled from class $z$. The denominator sums this over all classes, normalizing the result into a probability distribution. This posterior **is** a probabilistic clustering: instead of a single label, every point receives a full distribution over the $k$ clusters.

This is the difference between hard and soft clustering.

- **Hard clustering**, as in [$k$-means](/garden/ml/kMeans), assigns each $\mathbf{x}_i$ to exactly one cluster. We only ever learn the winning label.
- **Soft clustering**, as in mixture models, gives each $\mathbf{x}_i$ a distribution over clusters. A point sitting squarely inside one group is assigned to it with near certainty, while a point near the boundary between two groups splits its membership, which honestly captures our uncertainty about where it belongs.

### Maximum Likelihood and Why It Is Hard

To fit the model we use [maximum likelihood estimation](/garden/maths/probabilityStatistics/estimators): treat the probability of the data as a function of the parameters and maximize it. Under the iid assumption the likelihood factorizes over the data points, and taking logarithms turns the product into a sum, giving the **log-likelihood**:

$$
\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta}; \{\mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_n\}) = \sum_{i=1}^n \ln p(\mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \sum_{i=1}^n \ln \underbrace{\sum_{z=1}^k \pi_z \, p(\mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}_z)}_{\text{sum inside the log}}
$$

We then look for $\boldsymbol{\theta}^* = \arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \ell(\boldsymbol{\theta})$. The trouble is the **sum inside the logarithm**. In a model without latent variables the log would sit directly on a single density and the score equations would separate cleanly, often with a closed-form solution. Here the $\ln \sum_z$ couples all the parameters together: setting $\partial \ell / \partial \boldsymbol{\theta}_z = 0$ does not decouple by component, because every term in the outer sum depends on every component through the inner sum. There is no closed-form maximizer, so we need an iterative algorithm. That algorithm is **Expectation-Maximization**.

## The EM Algorithm

The whole difficulty was the logarithm of a sum. EM sidesteps it with a classic trick: replace the awkward log-of-a-sum by a tractable lower bound, then push that bound uphill. The lower bound comes from **Jensen's inequality**.

{{< callout type="info" title="Jensen's Inequality" >}}
For a convex function $\varphi$ and any random variable $X$,

$$
\varphi(\mathbb{E}[X]) \le \mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)]
$$

and the inequality reverses for a concave function. The function we care about is the logarithm, which is **concave**, so

$$
\ln \mathbb{E}[X] \ge \mathbb{E}[\ln X]
$$

Geometrically, a concave function lies above any of its chords, so the value at an average dominates the average of the values. This lets us move a logarithm inside an expectation at the cost of turning an equality into a lower bound.
{{< /callout >}}

To apply it we introduce, for each data point $\mathbf{x}_i$, a **variational distribution** $q_i$ over the latent class: numbers $q_{iz} \ge 0$ with $\sum_z q_{iz} = 1$. These are free parameters we get to choose. The trick is to multiply and divide the inner sum by $q_{iz}$, which turns the sum into an expectation under $q_i$:

$$
\ln p(\mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \ln \sum_{z} \pi_z \, p(\mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}_z) = \ln \sum_{z} q_{iz} \, \frac{\pi_z \, p(\mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}_z)}{q_{iz}} = \ln \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q_i}\!\left[ \frac{\pi_z \, p(\mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}_z)}{q_{iz}} \right]
$$

Now Jensen's inequality lets us pull the logarithm inside the expectation:

$$
\ln p(\mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}) \ge \sum_{z} q_{iz} \ln \frac{\pi_z \, p(\mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}_z)}{q_{iz}} = \sum_{z} q_{iz} \big[ \ln \pi_z + \ln p(\mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}_z) - \ln q_{iz} \big]
$$

Summing over all data points gives a lower bound on the full log-likelihood. This bound is the **Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO)**:

$$
\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \sum_{i=1}^n \ln \sum_{z=1}^k \pi_z \, p(\mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}_z) \;\ge\; \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{z=1}^k q_{iz} \big[ \ln \pi_z + \ln p(\mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}_z) - \ln q_{iz} \big] =: \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}, q)
$$

The bound is a parameterized family: each choice of the variational distributions $q$ gives a different lower bound. This is the same ELBO that appears in [variational inference](/garden/ml/bayesian/variationalInference#deriving-the-elbo) and in the [variational autoencoder](/garden/ml/computerVision/autoencoders#the-elbo-objective). The framing differs only in what plays the role of the latent and what we optimize. In variational inference the latent is the unknown parameter and the ELBO bounds the model evidence. Here the latent is the discrete class, and the ELBO bounds the data log-likelihood that we are maximizing over $\boldsymbol{\theta}$.

### How Tight Is the Bound?

To understand the bound, group the ELBO at a single point into two interpretable pieces. Split the bracket as $\ln \pi_z + \ln p(\mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}_z) = \ln\big(\pi_z p(\mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}_z)\big)$ and recall that $\pi_z p(\mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}_z) = p(\mathbf{x}_i, z; \boldsymbol{\theta})$ is the complete-data joint:

$$
\sum_z q_{iz} \ln \frac{p(\mathbf{x}_i, z; \boldsymbol{\theta})}{q_{iz}} = \underbrace{\sum_z q_{iz} \ln p(\mathbf{x}_i, z; \boldsymbol{\theta})}_{\text{expected complete log-likelihood}} \; + \; \underbrace{\Big(\!-\!\sum_z q_{iz} \ln q_{iz}\Big)}_{\text{entropy } H[q_i]}
$$

There is an even cleaner way to see the gap between $\ell$ and the bound. Using $p(\mathbf{x}_i, z) = p(z \mid \mathbf{x}_i) \, p(\mathbf{x}_i)$ inside the bound and rearranging gives the exact decomposition

$$
\ln p(\mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \underbrace{\sum_z q_{iz} \ln \frac{p(\mathbf{x}_i, z; \boldsymbol{\theta})}{q_{iz}}}_{\text{ELBO at } \mathbf{x}_i} \; + \; \underbrace{\text{KL}\big(q_i \,\big\|\, p(\cdot \mid \mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta})\big)}_{\ge 0}
$$

The log-likelihood equals the bound plus a [Kullback-Leibler divergence](/garden/ml/bayesian/variationalInference#kullback-leibler-divergence) between the variational distribution $q_i$ and the true posterior over the latent class. Since the KL divergence is never negative, the bound never exceeds $\ell$, which is exactly what "lower bound" means. More importantly, the bound is **tight** (equality holds) precisely when the KL term is zero, which happens when $q_i$ equals the posterior $p(\cdot \mid \mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta})$. This single observation drives the entire algorithm.

EM maximizes the ELBO by **coordinate ascent**, alternating between the two arguments of $\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}, q)$.

- The **E-step** maximizes the bound over the variational distributions $q$ with $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ held fixed. This tightens the bound.
- The **M-step** maximizes the bound over the parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ with $q$ held fixed. This improves the model fit.

### The E-step

With $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ fixed, we maximize $\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}, q)$ over each $q_i$ subject to the constraint $\sum_z q_{iz} = 1$. We already know the answer from the tightness condition: the optimal $q_i$ is the posterior. It is worth deriving it directly with a Lagrange multiplier, since the same calculation reappears for every mixture model.

{{< callout type="proof" title="The optimal variational distribution is the posterior" >}}
Fix a single data point and drop the index $i$. We maximize the per-point bound over $q$ subject to $\sum_z q_z = 1$. Writing $p_z := p(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta}_z)$ and introducing a Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$ for the constraint, the Lagrangian is

$$
\mathcal{L} = \sum_{z} q_z \big[ \ln \pi_z + \ln p_z - \ln q_z \big] - \lambda\Big( \sum_z q_z - 1 \Big)
$$

Differentiating with respect to $q_z$ and using $\frac{d}{dq_z}(q_z \ln q_z) = \ln q_z + 1$,

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial q_z} = \ln \pi_z + \ln p_z - \ln q_z - 1 - \lambda \overset{!}{=} 0 \quad\implies\quad q_z = e^{-(\lambda + 1)} \, \pi_z \, p_z
$$

The multiplier is fixed by the constraint $\sum_z q_z = 1$, which forces $e^{-(\lambda+1)} = 1 / \sum_\zeta \pi_\zeta p_\zeta$. Substituting back,

$$
q_z = \frac{\pi_z \, p(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta}_z)}{\sum_{\zeta} \pi_\zeta \, p(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta}_\zeta)} = P(Z = z \mid \mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta})
$$

which is exactly the latent class posterior.
{{< /callout >}}

So the E-step sets each variational distribution to the current posterior, called the **responsibility** of class $z$ for point $\mathbf{x}_i$:

$$
q_{iz} = \frac{\pi_z \, p(\mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}_z)}{\sum_{\zeta} \pi_\zeta \, p(\mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}_\zeta)} = P(Z_i = z \mid \mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta})
$$

The name is apt: $q_{iz}$ is the share of point $\mathbf{x}_i$ for which class $z$ is held responsible, and the shares sum to one across classes.

### The M-step

With the responsibilities fixed, the bound becomes a function of $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ alone, and the awkward coupling is gone. The $-\sum q_{iz} \ln q_{iz}$ entropy term does not involve $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, so we are left maximizing $\sum_i \sum_z q_{iz} [\ln \pi_z + \ln p(\mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}_z)]$, which splits cleanly into a part for the mixing proportions and a separate part for each component.

For the **mixing proportions**, maximize $\sum_i \sum_z q_{iz} \ln \pi_z$ subject to $\sum_z \pi_z = 1$. The same Lagrange-multiplier calculation as in the E-step gives

$$
\pi_z = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n q_{iz}
$$

The new mixing weight of class $z$ is its **average responsibility** across the data, that is the soft count of points assigned to it divided by $n$. It is the natural soft analogue of "the fraction of points in cluster $z$".

For the **component parameters**, the terms involving $\boldsymbol{\theta}_z$ are

$$
\boldsymbol{\theta}_z \;\leftarrow\; \arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_z} \sum_{i=1}^n q_{iz} \, \ln p(\mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}_z)
$$

This is a **weighted maximum likelihood** problem: the ordinary MLE for component $z$, except each data point is weighted by its responsibility $q_{iz}$. Two things make this tractable. First, the components decouple, so there is no coupling between classes to fight. Second, it is solvable whenever the ordinary (unweighted) MLE of the class-conditional is solvable, which it usually is for the simple distributions we choose as components. EM thus reduces a hard joint problem to a sequence of easy weighted ones.

### The Algorithm and Its Convergence

Putting the steps together gives the **EM algorithm**.

1. **Initialize** the parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}, \boldsymbol{\pi}^{(0)}$.
2. **Repeat until convergence:**
   - **E-step:** compute the responsibilities $q_{iz} = P(Z_i = z \mid \mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta})$.
   - **M-step:** update $\boldsymbol{\theta}, \boldsymbol{\pi}$ to maximize the ELBO given the responsibilities.
3. **Output** the parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}^*, \boldsymbol{\pi}^*$ and the soft assignments $q^*$.

The defining property of EM is that the **log-likelihood never decreases**. The reason is exactly the ELBO picture. The E-step raises the bound until it touches $\ell$ at the current $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ (the KL term hits zero), so after the E-step the bound equals the log-likelihood. The M-step then increases the bound, and since the log-likelihood always sits above the bound, it must increase by at least as much. Each iteration therefore pushes a quantity up that drags the log-likelihood up with it, and the algorithm is guaranteed to converge.

The catch is **where** it converges. EM finds a **local** maximum, not necessarily the global one, and which local maximum it reaches depends on the initialization and on whether the assumed model family actually fits the data. This is the price of dodging the intractable global problem. The standard defences are to run EM from several random initializations and keep the best, or to seed it with a cheaper method.

### $k$-means as a Special Case of EM

[$k$-means clustering](/garden/ml/kMeans) is the simplest instance of this framework: EM with **hard** assignments. Given data and $k$ centroids $\boldsymbol{\mu}_1, \dots, \boldsymbol{\mu}_k$, the two steps become

$$
\text{E-step:} \quad q_{iz} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } z = \arg\min_{\zeta} \|\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_\zeta\|^2 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
\qquad
\text{M-step:} \quad \boldsymbol{\mu}_z = \frac{\sum_{i} q_{iz} \, \mathbf{x}_i}{\sum_{i} q_{iz}}
$$

The E-step assigns each point entirely to its nearest centroid, a responsibility that is one for the winner and zero for everyone else, rather than a smooth distribution. The M-step then recomputes each centroid as the mean of the points assigned to it. This is precisely the hard limit of the Gaussian mixture EM we are about to derive. The limitations of $k$-means follow directly from these hard choices: it forces every point into exactly one cluster with no notion of uncertainty, it implicitly assumes equal-size spherical clusters, and it has no underlying probabilistic model, hence no likelihood for principled model selection. The fix for all three is to use a proper probabilistic model with soft assignments.

## Gaussian Mixture Models

The most popular mixture model for real-valued data takes each component to be a **multivariate Gaussian**, giving the **Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM)**. Each class $z$ has its own mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}_z$ and covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_z$, so $\boldsymbol{\theta}_z = (\boldsymbol{\mu}_z, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_z)$ and

$$
p(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\mu}_z, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_z) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\mu}_z, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_z) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2} \, \det(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_z)^{1/2}} \exp\!\left( -\tfrac{1}{2} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_z)^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_z^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_z) \right)
$$

The marginal $p(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \sum_z \pi_z \, \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\mu}_z, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_z)$ is a weighted sum of Gaussian bumps, which can model clusters of different locations, sizes, shapes, and orientations. A diagonal $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_z$ makes the coordinates conditionally independent and forces the bump to be axis-aligned, while a full covariance lets it tilt.

The Gaussian is the natural default for continuous data because of the [Central Limit Theorem](/garden/maths/probabilityStatistics/largeSamples): the average of many independent contributions tends to a Gaussian regardless of the shape of the individual contributions. Whenever an observed quantity is the aggregate of many small effects, as measurement noise typically is, a Gaussian is a principled model for it.

### EM for Gaussian Mixtures

Specializing the general EM updates to Gaussian components is mechanical, because the weighted MLE of a Gaussian is just as easy as its ordinary MLE. The **E-step** plugs the Gaussian density into the responsibility formula:

$$
q_{iz} = \frac{\pi_z \, \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\mu}_z, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_z)}{\sum_{\zeta} \pi_\zeta \, \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\mu}_\zeta, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_\zeta)}
$$

The **M-step** maximizes the responsibility-weighted Gaussian log-likelihood, which has the same closed form as fitting a single Gaussian, only with each point weighted by $q_{iz}$. Writing $n_z = \sum_{i=1}^n q_{iz}$ for the soft count of class $z$, the updates are

$$
\pi_z = \frac{n_z}{n}, \qquad
\boldsymbol{\mu}_z = \frac{1}{n_z} \sum_{i=1}^n q_{iz} \, \mathbf{x}_i, \qquad
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_z = \frac{1}{n_z} \sum_{i=1}^n q_{iz} \, (\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_z)(\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_z)^\top
$$

Each update is the soft version of a familiar estimator. The mixing weight is the soft fraction of points in the cluster, the mean is the responsibility-weighted average of the data, and the covariance is the responsibility-weighted scatter around the new mean. Points the cluster is confident about pull its parameters strongly, while points it barely claims have little say.

This makes the relationship to $k$-means concrete. A GMM generalizes $k$-means by giving each cluster a mixing weight and a full covariance, and by using soft responsibilities instead of hard assignments. Shrink every covariance towards $\sigma^2 \mathbf{I}$ with $\sigma^2 \to 0$ and the responsibilities sharpen to hard assignments, the mean update becomes the centroid update, and the GMM collapses back to $k$-means.

{{< callout type="example" title="Soft clustering of customers" >}}
Suppose each customer is described by two features, the number of purchases and the annual spending, and we fit a GMM with three components by EM. The fitted model places three Gaussian ellipses over the data, one per segment, each with its own centre, spread, and orientation. A customer in the heart of one ellipse is assigned to that segment with responsibility close to one, while a customer sitting between two segments receives a split like $(0.6, 0.4, 0.0)$. To turn this into a hard clustering we simply take $\arg\max_z q_{iz}$, but the soft responsibilities carry strictly more information, namely the model's confidence at every point.
{{< /callout >}}

### Practical Considerations

Three issues come up when running GMM EM in practice.

- **Initialization matters.** Because EM only finds a local maximum, the starting point shapes the result. Common strategies are to initialize with [$k$-means](/garden/ml/kMeans) (or $k$-means++), to use several random restarts, and to keep the run with the highest final log-likelihood.
- **Choosing $k$.** The number of components is a hyperparameter, not something EM selects for you. Since adding components always increases the likelihood, raw likelihood cannot choose $k$. Penalized criteria like the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) or the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), or cross-validation on held-out log-likelihood, trade fit against complexity.
- **Numerical stability.** Responsibilities involve ratios of tiny exponentials, so compute them with the log-sum-exp trick to avoid underflow. Watch for **singularities**: if a component collapses onto a single point, its covariance shrinks, $\det(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_z) \to 0$, and the likelihood diverges to infinity. Regularizing the covariance with a small added diagonal term keeps it well-conditioned.

## Summary

A latent variable model explains observed data $\mathbf{x}$ through an unobserved cause $\mathbf{z}$, specifying a simple complete-data model $p(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z})$ and deriving the marginal $p(\mathbf{x})$ by summing out the latent. The mixture model is the canonical instance, with a categorical latent that turns the model into probabilistic clustering through the posterior $P(Z = z \mid \mathbf{x})$. Maximum likelihood is hard because of the sum inside the logarithm, so we maximize the ELBO instead, a Jensen lower bound that becomes tight when the variational distribution equals the posterior. EM exploits this by coordinate ascent: the E-step sets the responsibilities to the posterior, and the M-step solves a weighted MLE for the parameters, with the log-likelihood guaranteed to increase each round towards a local optimum. The Gaussian mixture model is the workhorse special case, generalizing $k$-means to soft assignments with per-cluster covariances. The same EM machinery, applied to count data, gives the topic and matrix-factorization models developed in the [topic models](/garden/ml/topicModels) note.
