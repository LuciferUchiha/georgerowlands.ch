A topic model is a [latent variable model](/garden/ml/latentVariableModels) for text. Where a [Gaussian mixture](/garden/ml/latentVariableModels#gaussian-mixture-models) explains real-valued vectors through hidden clusters, a topic model explains the words in a collection of documents through hidden **topics**: latent themes like "sport", "politics", or "medicine" that a document draws on in some proportion. The promise is to uncover the thematic structure of a large corpus automatically, without any labels, and the mechanism is the same Expectation-Maximization machinery we have already built, now applied to word counts.

This note develops topic models in three stages. First the **probabilistic latent semantic analysis (pLSA)** model and its EM training, which is a mixture model over word occurrences. Then **latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA)**, which fixes pLSA's inability to handle new documents by placing a Bayesian prior over topic proportions. Finally the **probabilistic matrix decomposition** view, which reveals that pLSA is a non-negative low-rank factorization of the count matrix and connects it to [non-negative matrix factorization](/garden/ml/matrixCompletion#non-negative-matrix-factorization).

## Representing a Document Collection

We are given a collection of $n$ documents drawn from a vocabulary of $m$ distinct words $\{w_1, \dots, w_m\}$. Document $i$ has length $s_i$, and we write $x_{it} \in \{w_1, \dots, w_m\}$ for the word at position $t = 1, \dots, s_i$ in document $i$. The first modelling decision is how much of this structure to keep.

A topic model makes the **bag-of-words** assumption: the "aboutness" of a document is captured by which words it uses and how often, irrespective of their order. Formally this is **exchangeability**, the assumption that the joint distribution of the words in a document is invariant under any permutation of their positions. Exchangeability is weaker than independence, but it is enough to let us discard word order and keep only counts. This is a crude assumption ("the cat sat on the mat" and "the mat sat on the cat" become identical), and it is exactly what later sequence models like [transformers](/garden/ml/llms/transformers) abandon, but it is what makes classical topic models tractable.

Under exchangeability the **minimal sufficient statistic** is the matrix of word counts. Let $N_{ij}$ be the number of times word $w_j$ occurs in document $i$,

$$
N_{ij} = \big|\{ t : x_{it} = w_j \}\big|, \qquad \mathbf{N} = (N_{ij}) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{n \times m}
$$

This **term-document matrix** is the same object that underlies [TF-IDF](/garden/ml/tfidf) and document retrieval, with documents as rows and vocabulary words as columns. Everything a topic model needs to know about the corpus is contained in $\mathbf{N}$. In practice one ignores words that never occur and usually removes stopwords and very rare words before building it.

### Topics as Latent Variables

What should a topic be attached to? Attaching one topic to an entire document would just be document clustering, forcing each document into a single theme. Attaching topics to individual words in the vocabulary would be word clustering. Neither captures the key fact that a single document mixes themes: an article about the 2022 soccer world cup in a particular country might combine soccer vocabulary ("teams", "match"), political vocabulary ("labour", "president"), and medical terms ("pandemic", "Covid").

The resolution is to attach a latent topic variable to **each word occurrence**. The complete-data model is a pair $(X_{it}, Z_{it})$ for every position, where $X_{it} \in \{w_1, \dots, w_m\}$ is the observed word and $Z_{it} \in \{1, \dots, k\}$ is its hidden topic. A document is then characterized by a distribution over topics, and each topic by a distribution over words. This gives the two ingredients of a topic model:

$$
p(z \mid d_i) \;\;\text{: the topic proportions of document } i, \qquad
p(w_j \mid z) \;\;\text{: the word distribution of topic } z
$$

These define a **two-stage sampling** process for each of the $s_i$ slots in document $i$: first draw a topic $z \sim p(z \mid d_i)$ from the document's topic proportions, then draw a word $w \sim p(w \mid z)$ from that topic's word distribution. A document whose proportions are $p(z \mid d_i) = (0.1, 0.2, 0.4, \dots)$ leans mostly on topic 3, and each word it contains is generated by first picking one of these topics and then sampling a word from it.

## Probabilistic Latent Semantic Analysis

Marginalizing the topic out of the two-stage process gives the probability of seeing word $w_j$ at any position of document $i$ as a mixture over topics, with the document's proportions as the mixing weights:

$$
p(w_j \mid d_i) = \sum_{z=1}^k p(w_j \mid z) \, p(z \mid d_i)
$$

This is the **probabilistic latent semantic analysis (pLSA)** model, and the structure is precisely a [mixture model](/garden/ml/latentVariableModels#mixture-models): for each document the word distribution is a convex combination of the $k$ topic word-distributions, mixed by that document's topic proportions. Because the counts are the sufficient statistic, the log-likelihood of the whole corpus weights each distinct word by how often it occurs:

$$
\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta}; \mathbf{N}) = \ln p(\mathbf{N}; \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \sum_{i,j} N_{ij} \, \ln p(w_j \mid d_i) = \sum_{i,j} N_{ij} \, \ln \sum_{z=1}^k p(w_j \mid z) \, p(z \mid d_i)
$$

The parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ are the word distributions $p(w \mid z)$ and the topic proportions $p(z \mid d)$, with no further constraints tying documents together, which is why pLSA is called a **saturated** model. We are back to the same obstacle as in any mixture model: a sum inside the logarithm with no closed-form maximizer. The cure is the same too, [EM](/garden/ml/latentVariableModels#the-em-algorithm).

### EM for pLSA

We introduce a variational distribution $q_{itz}$ for every word occurrence, the responsibility of topic $z$ for the word at position $t$ in document $i$. Applying Jensen's inequality exactly as in the general derivation gives the ELBO

$$
\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \ge \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{t=1}^{s_i} \sum_{z=1}^k q_{itz} \big[ \ln p(x_{it} \mid z) + \ln p(z \mid d_i) - \ln q_{itz} \big]
$$

and coordinate ascent on it yields the two steps. The **E-step** sets each responsibility to the topic posterior for that occurrence,

$$
q_{itz} = \frac{p(x_{it} \mid z) \, p(z \mid d_i)}{\sum_{\zeta} p(x_{it} \mid \zeta) \, p(\zeta \mid d_i)}
$$

The **M-step** solves the weighted MLE for the two sets of probabilities. Both updates are intuitive **reweighting** of the counts by the responsibilities:

$$
p(w_j \mid z) = \frac{\sum_{i,t} \mathbb{I}[x_{it} = w_j] \, q_{itz}}{\sum_{i,t} q_{itz}}, \qquad
p(z \mid d_i) = \frac{1}{s_i} \sum_{t=1}^{s_i} q_{itz}
$$

A topic's word distribution is the share of occurrences of each word that were assigned to that topic, normalized over the vocabulary. A document's topic proportions are the average responsibility of each topic across that document's positions. As always with EM, we alternate the two steps and the log-likelihood climbs monotonically to a local optimum.

{{< callout type="warning" title="pLSA cannot handle new documents" >}}
The topic proportions $p(z \mid d_i)$ are fit individually for each document in the training corpus. There is one free proportion vector per document and nothing links them, so the number of parameters grows with the corpus. When a genuinely new document arrives, the model has no proportion vector for it and no mechanism to produce one, since the proportions were point estimates tied to the training documents. pLSA describes the corpus it was trained on but does not define a generative process for documents in general. Fixing this is the motivation for LDA.
{{< /callout >}}

## Latent Dirichlet Allocation

To turn pLSA into a proper generative model, **latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA)** treats the topic proportions of a document not as parameters to be fit, but as a random vector drawn from a prior. Rename the two ingredients as matrices for convenience,

$$
u_{jz} := p(w_j \mid z), \qquad v_{zi} := p(z \mid d_i)
$$

so the columns of $\mathbf{U}$ are the topic word-distributions and the topic-proportion vector of a document is a point $\mathbf{v}$ on the probability simplex, $v_z \ge 0$ and $\sum_z v_z = 1$. The key idea is to define a **distribution over these mixture vectors**, so that each document's proportions are sampled rather than fixed. We need a distribution over the simplex, and the natural choice comes from conjugacy.

### Conjugacy and the Dirichlet Distribution

In [Bayesian inference](/garden/ml/bayesian/bayesianLearning) we combine a prior $p(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ with a likelihood $p(X \mid \boldsymbol{\theta})$ to get a posterior $p(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid X) \propto p(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \, p(X \mid \boldsymbol{\theta})$. A prior is **conjugate** to a likelihood when the posterior belongs to the same family as the prior. Conjugacy is desirable because the posterior then has the same closed form as the prior, with updated parameters, and Bayesian updating reduces to bookkeeping on those parameters.

The topic proportions are the parameters of a **categorical** distribution over topics (each word's topic is drawn from $\mathbf{v}$). The conjugate prior for the categorical (and its repeated-trial version, the multinomial) is the **Dirichlet distribution**, the multivariate generalization of the Beta distribution. On the simplex it has density

$$
\text{Dir}(\mathbf{v}; \boldsymbol{\alpha}) = \frac{1}{B(\boldsymbol{\alpha})} \prod_{z=1}^k v_z^{\alpha_z - 1}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\alpha} \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}^k
$$

where $B(\boldsymbol{\alpha})$ is the normalizing constant and $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ is the **concentration parameter**. The concentration controls how the mass spreads over the simplex: $\alpha_z = 1$ for all $z$ is uniform over the simplex, $\alpha_z < 1$ pushes mass towards the corners and yields sparse proportions (a document about few topics), and $\alpha_z > 1$ concentrates mass near the centre (a document blending many topics). One typically fixes a symmetric $\alpha_z = \alpha$, such as $\alpha = 1/k$, or optimizes it on held-out data.

{{< callout type="proof" title="The Dirichlet is conjugate to the categorical" >}}
Let $\mathbf{v}$ be a topic-proportion vector with a Dirichlet prior $\text{Dir}(\mathbf{v}; \boldsymbol{\alpha})$, and suppose we observe topic assignments whose counts are $m_z$ (the number of times topic $z$ was drawn). The categorical likelihood of these assignments is $\prod_z v_z^{m_z}$. Multiplying prior and likelihood,

$$
p(\mathbf{v} \mid X) \propto \underbrace{\prod_{z} v_z^{\alpha_z - 1}}_{\text{prior}} \cdot \underbrace{\prod_{z} v_z^{m_z}}_{\text{likelihood}} = \prod_{z} v_z^{(\alpha_z + m_z) - 1}
$$

which is again a Dirichlet, $\text{Dir}(\mathbf{v}; \boldsymbol{\alpha} + \mathbf{m})$. The posterior stays in the Dirichlet family and updating just adds the observed counts to the concentration, so the prior pseudo-counts $\alpha_z$ act like topics seen before any data. This is the multivariate version of the Beta being conjugate to the Bernoulli, recovered when $k = 2$.
{{< /callout >}}

### LDA as a Bayesian Topic Model

LDA augments the topic model with a Dirichlet prior over each document's proportions and then **integrates them out**. The marginal probability of a document $X = (x_1, \dots, x_s)$ given the global word-topic matrix $\mathbf{U}$ and the concentration $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ is

$$
P(X = (x_1, \dots, x_s); \mathbf{U}) = \int \underbrace{\prod_{t=1}^{s} p(x_t \mid \mathbf{U}, \mathbf{v})}_{\text{categorical}} \; \underbrace{p(\mathbf{v} \mid \boldsymbol{\alpha})}_{\text{Dirichlet}} \, d\mathbf{v}, \qquad p(x_t = w_j \mid \mathbf{U}, \mathbf{v}) = \sum_{z=1}^k u_{jz} \, v_z
$$

The change from pLSA is decisive. The word-topic matrix $\mathbf{U}$ is **global**, shared across the whole corpus, whereas the proportion vector $\mathbf{v}$ is document-specific and **integrated out** rather than fit. The model therefore defines a probability for any document, including unseen ones: to score a new document we use the shared $\mathbf{U}$ and marginalize over its unknown proportions under the Dirichlet prior. The number of parameters no longer grows with the corpus, which both prevents overfitting and lets the model generalize. Intuitively, LDA adds a distribution on top of the document-level distribution that pLSA already had, which is what makes it a hierarchical Bayesian model.

The price is that the integral over $\mathbf{v}$ has no closed form, so learning LDA is harder than the clean EM of pLSA. Practical algorithms include Markov chain Monte Carlo (collapsed Gibbs sampling) and [variational inference](/garden/ml/bayesian/variationalInference) (variational EM, online variants), all of which scale to large corpora. LDA, introduced by Blei, Ng, and Jordan, is the more principled and widely adopted topic model: it incorporates Bayesian priors to prevent overfitting and generalize to unseen documents, and it produces interpretable topic distributions.

## The Matrix Factorization View

Topic models have a second face as **matrix factorization**, which links them to [matrix completion](/garden/ml/matrixCompletion) and to the [word embeddings](/garden/ml/llms/textEmbeddings) developed elsewhere. Recall the pLSA parameters $u_{jz} = p(w_j \mid z)$ and $v_{zi} = p(z \mid d_i)$. The predicted word distribution of document $i$ is

$$
p(w_j \mid d_i) = \sum_{z=1}^k u_{jz} \, v_{zi} = (\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V})_{ij} =: \hat{N}_{ij}
$$

so the matrix of predicted frequencies is the **product** $\hat{\mathbf{N}} = \mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}$. Since the inner dimension is the number of topics $k$, this product has rank at most $k$, and pLSA is a low-rank approximation to the data. The subtlety is that $\hat{\mathbf{N}}$ approximates **relative** frequencies, not raw counts: the observed count relates to the prediction through the document length $s_i$,

$$
N_{ij} \approx s_i \, \hat{N}_{ij}
$$

The probabilistic interpretation imposes two constraints that an unconstrained factorization would not have. First, **non-negativity**: $u_{jz} \ge 0$ and $v_{zi} \ge 0$, because they are probabilities. Second, **normalization**: each topic's word distribution sums to one over the vocabulary, $\sum_j u_{jz} = 1$, and each document's topic proportions sum to one over topics, $\sum_z v_{zi} = 1$. Maximizing the pLSA log-likelihood $\sum_{i,j} N_{ij} \ln \hat{N}_{ij}$ subject to these constraints is therefore a constrained, non-negative, low-rank matrix factorization with a likelihood (equivalently a KL divergence) objective.

This is exactly **non-negative matrix factorization (NMF)** with a particular loss. Dropping the normalization constraints and keeping only non-negativity gives NMF as a standalone model, which can be paired with either the likelihood objective above or a simpler squared-error objective. Because NMF is a matrix-factorization method that sits naturally alongside the low-rank theory and alternating least squares already developed there, its standalone treatment lives in the [matrix completion note](/garden/ml/matrixCompletion#non-negative-matrix-factorization). The takeaway here is that a topic model is a special case of non-negative matrix factorization with a log-likelihood objective.

{{< callout type="info" title="NMF versus LDA" >}}
Both decompose the term-document matrix into non-negative factors, but they sit at different points on the simplicity-principle trade-off. NMF is fast, simple, and interpretable, with no priors and no integrals, and it is used in many practical topic-modelling pipelines. LDA is a fully Bayesian model with a Dirichlet prior, which costs more to fit but generalizes to unseen documents and guards against overfitting. NMF asks only for a good non-negative reconstruction of the counts, while LDA asks for a generative story for documents in general.
{{< /callout >}}

## Summary

A topic model is a latent variable model for the term-document matrix, attaching a hidden topic to every word occurrence under the bag-of-words (exchangeability) assumption. pLSA is a mixture model over occurrences, fit by EM whose E-step computes topic posteriors and whose M-step reweights word counts, but it cannot score new documents because its topic proportions are per-document parameters. LDA repairs this by placing a Dirichlet prior, the conjugate of the categorical, over the proportions and integrating them out, leaving a global word-topic matrix that generalizes across documents at the cost of an intractable integral. Viewed as linear algebra, pLSA factorizes the relative-frequency matrix as a non-negative low-rank product $\hat{\mathbf{N}} = \mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}$, making it a special case of [non-negative matrix factorization](/garden/ml/matrixCompletion#non-negative-matrix-factorization). The same co-occurrence-into-geometry idea, applied to words rather than documents, gives the [word embeddings](/garden/ml/llms/textEmbeddings) of word2vec and GloVe.
