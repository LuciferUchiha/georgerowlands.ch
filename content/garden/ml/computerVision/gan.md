A **generative model** learns from a set of training samples drawn from an unknown data distribution $p(\mathbf{x})$ and tries to reproduce it by learning a model distribution $p_\theta(\mathbf{x})$ that we can draw new samples from. The aim is for $p_\theta$ to be indistinguishable from $p$, so that generated samples look just like real data.

{{< figure
    src="/images/ml/imageGenerationRealFake.jpg"
    caption="One of these faces is a real photograph and the other is generated by a GAN. The aim of generative modeling is exactly this kind of indistinguishability."
    alt="A real face photograph next to a GAN-generated face"
    width="480"
>}}

The standard approach in machine learning is to fit such a model by **maximum likelihood**, choosing the parameters $\theta$ that make the observed data as probable as possible under the model, $\arg\max_\theta \log p_\theta(\mathbf{x})$. Most generative models follow this principle. Autoregressive models factor the likelihood with the chain rule, normalizing flows compute it exactly through invertible maps, and latent-variable models such as the [variational autoencoder](/garden/ml/computerVision/autoencoders#variational-autoencoders) and [diffusion models](/garden/ml/computerVision/diffusion) optimize a tractable lower bound on it (the ELBO). It works, but it forces compromises. The likelihood has to stay tractable, which limits the structure of the model, and the lower-bound approaches optimize an approximate surrogate rather than the likelihood itself.

**Generative adversarial networks (GANs)**, introduced by Goodfellow et al. in 2014, are a completely new and influential idea. The idea is to drop the likelihood entirely and instead of asking "how probable is the data under my model?", a GAN sets up a game between two competing networks and lets the competition itself drive $p_\theta$ toward $p$.

## The Adversarial Game

The setup is best understood as a contest between a **counterfeiter** and a **detective**. The counterfeiter produces fake samples and tries to pass them off as real, while the detective tries to tell real samples from fakes. As the detective gets sharper, the counterfeiter is forced to produce more convincing fakes, and the two improve until the fakes can no longer be told apart from the real thing. The two players are neural networks, parameterized by weight vectors $\theta$ and $\phi$ respectively:

- The counterfeiter is the **generator** $G_\theta: \mathbb{R}^k \to \mathbb{R}^D$, a network with weights $\theta$ that maps a noise vector $\mathbf{z} \sim p(\mathbf{z})$ (typically $\mathcal{N}(0, \mathbf{I})$) to a sample $\mathbf{x} = G_\theta(\mathbf{z})$. It is an ordinary [neural network](/garden/ml/neuralNetworks), for example a stack of linear layers with nonlinear activations, or transposed convolutions when generating images. Passing the fixed noise distribution through $G_\theta$ induces the model distribution $p_\theta$.
- The detective is the **discriminator** $D_\phi: \mathbb{R}^D \to [0, 1]$, a network with weights $\phi$ ending in a sigmoid, that acts as a binary classifier outputting the probability that an input is real rather than generated.

### Generation as Classification

To turn this contest into a concrete objective we frame it as a **binary classification problem**. Imagine a single dataset built by flipping a fair coin for each example. On heads we draw a real sample from $p(\mathbf{x})$, on tails we draw a generated sample from $p_\theta(\mathbf{x})$. Let $Y \in \{0, 1\}$ indicate which source was used, with $Y = 1$ for real and $Y = 0$ for generated. The joint distribution of an example and its label is the mixture

$$
p(\mathbf{x}, Y; \theta) = \underbrace{\frac{Y}{2}\, p(\mathbf{x})}_{\text{real, } Y = 1} + \underbrace{\frac{1 - Y}{2}\, p_\theta(\mathbf{x})}_{\text{generated, } Y = 0}, \qquad Y \in \{0, 1\}
$$

where the two $\tfrac{1}{2}$ factors are the coin probabilities $P(Y = 1) = P(Y = 0) = \tfrac{1}{2}$. The discriminator is a classifier on this dataset, and we score it with the **classification log-likelihood** used in [logistic regression](/garden/ml/linearClassificationlogisticRegression),

$$
\ell(\theta, \phi) = \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x}, Y) \sim p(\mathbf{x}, Y; \theta)}\big[\, Y \ln D_\phi(\mathbf{x}) + (1 - Y)\ln(1 - D_\phi(\mathbf{x}))\,\big]
$$

Taking the expectation over the coin flip $Y$ splits this into a real term and a generated term, each weighted by $\tfrac{1}{2}$,

$$
\ell(\theta, \phi) = \frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x} \sim p}\big[\ln D_\phi(\mathbf{x})\big] + \frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x} \sim p_\theta}\big[\ln(1 - D_\phi(\mathbf{x}))\big]
$$

The first term rewards the discriminator for assigning high probability to real data and does not depend on $\theta$ at all. The second term is the only one the generator can influence. The discriminator wants this quantity **high** (it is classifying well), the generator wants it **low** (its samples are being called real). Putting both wishes together gives a single **minimax** (saddle point) objective, in which the generator minimizes over $\theta$ exactly what the discriminator maximizes over $\phi$,

$$
\underbrace{\min_{\theta}}_{\text{generator}}\ \underbrace{\max_{\phi}}_{\text{discriminator}}\ \ell(\theta, \phi)
$$

The inner maximization sharpens the discriminator against the current generator, which is the worst case for the generator, and the outer minimization then improves the generator against that sharpened opponent. The generator and discriminator are **adversaries** optimizing one shared objective in opposite directions, which is where the name comes from.

This is a two-player game, and its solution is a **Nash equilibrium**: a pair $(\theta^*, \phi^*)$ at which neither player can improve its own objective by changing only its own parameters. At the ideal equilibrium the generator has matched the data, $p_\theta = p$, and the discriminator is reduced to guessing, $D_\phi = \tfrac{1}{2}$, because real and generated samples are genuinely indistinguishable.

## The Optimal Discriminator and the Jensen-Shannon Divergence

Is this game actually pushing $p_\theta$ toward $p$ in a principled way, or is it just a heuristic? To answer this we examine the **inner maximization**. For a fixed generator, what is the best possible discriminator, and what does the generator then end up minimizing against it?

For a fixed generator the discriminator faces an ordinary probabilistic classification problem: given a point $\mathbf{x}$, report how likely it is to be real rather than generated. The best any classifier can do is output the **true probability** of that event, the **class posterior** $P\{Y = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}\}$. Reporting anything else is penalized by the classification log-likelihood, which is a proper score maximized only by the true probabilities. We can confirm this and read off the posterior by maximizing the objective at each point separately.

{{< callout type="proof" title="The optimal discriminator is the class posterior" >}}
Write the objective as a single integral, abbreviating $a = \tfrac{1}{2}p(\mathbf{x})$ and $b = \tfrac{1}{2}p_\theta(\mathbf{x})$,

$$
\ell(\theta, \phi) = \int \Big[\, \tfrac{1}{2}p(\mathbf{x}) \ln D_\phi(\mathbf{x}) + \tfrac{1}{2}p_\theta(\mathbf{x})\ln(1 - D_\phi(\mathbf{x})) \,\Big]\, d\mathbf{x}
$$

The value of $D_\phi$ at one point $\mathbf{x}$ does not affect the integrand at any other point, so we can maximize the bracket $a \ln D + b \ln(1 - D)$ over $D \in (0, 1)$ separately for each $\mathbf{x}$. Setting the derivative to zero,

$$
\frac{a}{D} - \frac{b}{1 - D} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a(1 - D) = bD \quad\Longrightarrow\quad D = \frac{a}{a + b} = \frac{p(\mathbf{x})}{p(\mathbf{x}) + p_\theta(\mathbf{x})}
$$
{{< /callout >}}

The maximizer is the same thing [Bayes' rule](/garden/ml/bayesian/bayesianLearning/#bayesian-decision-theory) gives for the posterior probability that a sample is real. With equal priors $P(Y = 1) = P(Y = 0) = \tfrac{1}{2}$ and class-conditional densities $p(\mathbf{x} \mid Y = 1) = p(\mathbf{x})$ and $p(\mathbf{x} \mid Y = 0) = p_\theta(\mathbf{x})$,

$$
D^*(\mathbf{x}) = P\{Y = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}\} = \frac{p(\mathbf{x}) \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}}{p(\mathbf{x}) \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} + p_\theta(\mathbf{x}) \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}} = \frac{p(\mathbf{x})}{p(\mathbf{x}) + p_\theta(\mathbf{x})}
$$

This is the **Bayes-optimal classifier**, and it is intuitive. Where real data is much more likely than generated data, $D^*$ is close to $1$. Where the generator already matches the data, $p_\theta(\mathbf{x}) \approx p(\mathbf{x})$ and $D^*(\mathbf{x}) \approx \tfrac{1}{2}$, the classifier is reduced to guessing.

There is a catch, and it is the one that defines GANs in practice. The optimal discriminator $D^*$ is written in terms of the true density $p$, which is exactly the thing we do not have, so we cannot compute it in closed form. This is the reason the discriminator is a learned network $D_\phi$ and the reason the game carries the inner maximization $\max_\phi$: optimizing over a family of networks is our tractable way of chasing $D^*$. We never reach it exactly, but imagining the discriminator at its optimum lets us read off what the generator is really being driven toward, which is what we do next.

### What the Generator Ends Up Minimizing

Suppose the discriminator has reached its optimum $D^*$. What objective is the generator left minimizing? The answer is a **divergence**, a number that measures how different two distributions are. Two divergences are relevant here.

The [Kullback-Leibler divergence](/garden/ml/bayesian/variationalinference#kullback-leibler-divergence) between two densities is

$$
\text{KL}(p \,\|\, q) = \int p(\mathbf{x})\ln\frac{p(\mathbf{x})}{q(\mathbf{x})}\, d\mathbf{x} = \mathbb{E}_p[\ln p(\mathbf{X}) - \ln q(\mathbf{X})] \ge 0
$$

It is **not symmetric**: in general $\text{KL}(p \,\|\, q) \neq \text{KL}(q \,\|\, p)$, because the log-ratio is averaged under the first argument $p$ alone. The two directions even penalize different mistakes. $\text{KL}(p \,\|\, q)$ is large wherever $p$ has mass but $q$ does not, punishing a model $q$ that fails to cover part of the real data, whereas $\text{KL}(q \,\|\, p)$ punishes a model that puts mass where there is no data. KL is also **unbounded**, and in fact becomes infinite the moment $q$ assigns zero probability somewhere $p$ does not. That is exactly the disjoint-support situation we run into with high-dimensional data, and it makes a one-directional KL a poor score for indistinguishability, where we would prefer a single symmetric, finite number that does not depend on which distribution we name first.

The **Jensen-Shannon divergence** is built to fix both problems. Rather than comparing $p$ and $q$ to each other, it compares each of them to their average $m = \tfrac{1}{2}(p + q)$ and averages the two directions,

$$
\text{JS}(p \,\|\, q) = \frac{1}{2}\text{KL}\big(p \,\|\, m\big) + \frac{1}{2}\text{KL}\big(q \,\|\, m\big)
$$

Because the mixture $m$ is never zero where either $p$ or $q$ is positive, each KL term stays finite, so JS is **bounded**, with $0 \le \text{JS}(p \,\|\, q) \le \ln 2$. Because the construction treats $p$ and $q$ the same way, swapping them leaves it unchanged, so JS is **symmetric**. It is zero exactly when $p = q$.

Now we can answer the question. Substitute the optimal discriminator $D^*(\mathbf{x}) = \tfrac{p(\mathbf{x})}{p(\mathbf{x}) + p_\theta(\mathbf{x})}$, and its complement $1 - D^*(\mathbf{x}) = \tfrac{p_\theta(\mathbf{x})}{p(\mathbf{x}) + p_\theta(\mathbf{x})}$, into the objective $\ell$,

$$
\ell(\theta) = \frac{1}{2}\int p(\mathbf{x})\ln\frac{p(\mathbf{x})}{p(\mathbf{x}) + p_\theta(\mathbf{x})}\, d\mathbf{x} + \frac{1}{2}\int p_\theta(\mathbf{x})\ln\frac{p_\theta(\mathbf{x})}{p(\mathbf{x}) + p_\theta(\mathbf{x})}\, d\mathbf{x}
$$

Each denominator is $p + p_\theta = 2m$, where $m = \tfrac{1}{2}(p + p_\theta)$ is the mixture. Pulling the factor of two out of each logarithm, $\ln\frac{a}{2m} = \ln\frac{a}{m} - \ln 2$, turns each integral into a KL divergence to the mixture and leaves a constant $-\tfrac{1}{2}\ln 2$ behind from each term,

$$
\ell(\theta) = \frac{1}{2}\underbrace{\int p\,\ln\frac{p}{m}\, d\mathbf{x}}_{\text{KL}(p \,\|\, m)} + \frac{1}{2}\underbrace{\int p_\theta\,\ln\frac{p_\theta}{m}\, d\mathbf{x}}_{\text{KL}(p_\theta \,\|\, m)} - \ln 2 = \text{JS}(p \,\|\, p_\theta) - \ln 2
$$

So against the optimal discriminator the generator is minimizing the **Jensen-Shannon divergence** $\text{JS}(p \,\|\, p_\theta)$, since the additive constant $-\ln 2$ does not move the minimizer. The best the generator can do is reach $p_\theta = p$, where $\text{JS} = 0$ and the objective hits its global optimum $\ell = -\ln 2$, exactly the point where the discriminator collapses to $D^* \equiv \tfrac{1}{2}$ and can no longer tell the two apart. This is the theoretical payoff. The adversarial game is not an arbitrary heuristic, it is a way of driving down a real, symmetric divergence between the model and the data, a different criterion from the maximum likelihood we started with.

## Training Difficulties

The minimax formulation is elegant, but optimizing it is notoriously hard. We look at three failure modes and their remedies.

### Mode Collapse

The first failure is not special to GANs. Any generative model can suffer **mode collapse**, where it captures only some of the modes (the separated regions of high probability) of the data distribution and ignores the rest, sacrificing diversity for a narrow slice it can reproduce well.

In a GAN this shows up as the generator mapping many different noise vectors $\mathbf{z}$ to the same few outputs, in the extreme producing almost the same image no matter what noise it is given. The reason the adversarial objective allows this is that a handful of very realistic outputs is enough to fool the discriminator. Nothing in the objective directly rewards diversity, so a generator that has found one convincing image has little pressure to find the others.

This is the **mode-seeking** end of a spectrum. A mode-seeking model locks onto a subset of the data's modes and reproduces them sharply, whereas a **mode-covering** model spreads its mass across all the modes at the cost of also placing probability in the low-density gaps between them. GANs tend to be mode-seeking, which is why they can produce sharp but not always diverse samples, in contrast to likelihood-trained models that lean mode-covering and produce blurrier averages. Mode collapse is the hardest of the three failures to cure. Architectural and training tricks help, but the most principled remedy is to change the divergence being minimized, which is the motivation for the Wasserstein GAN below.

### Vanishing Gradients

Recall which player owns which part of the shared objective. Of the two terms in $\ell$, only the second depends on the generator, so the generator's entire job is to minimize

$$
\theta \xrightarrow{\ \min\ } \int \ln(1 - D_\phi(\mathbf{x}))\, p_\theta(\mathbf{x})\, d\mathbf{x}
$$

the expected log-probability the discriminator assigns to "generated" on the generator's own samples. Intuitively the generator wants its samples to be called real, that is it wants to push $D_\phi(\mathbf{x})$ toward $1$, which sends $\ln(1 - D_\phi(\mathbf{x}))$ toward $-\infty$.

The trouble appears at the start of training. The generator is poor, so its samples are easy to spot and the discriminator quickly becomes almost perfect, driving $D_\phi(\mathbf{x}) \to 0$ on generated samples. This imbalance between a strong discriminator and a weak generator is the problem. Near $D_\phi(\mathbf{x}) = 0$ the term $\ln(1 - D_\phi(\mathbf{x}))$ is flat, so its gradient nearly vanishes exactly when the generator most needs a learning signal.

The standard fix is to use a **non-saturating objective**. Instead of minimizing $\ln(1 - D_\phi)$, the generator maximizes $\ln D_\phi$,

$$
\theta \xrightarrow{\ \max\ } \int \ln D_\phi(\mathbf{x})\, p_\theta(\mathbf{x})\, d\mathbf{x}
$$

which shares the same fixed point but provides a strong gradient precisely when $D_\phi(\mathbf{x})$ is small. This is what is used in practice. It no longer minimizes the Jensen-Shannon divergence exactly, but it trains far more stably.

### Oscillation and Non-Convergence

Minimizing a loss with gradient descent reliably decreases it, but a minimax saddle point has no such guarantee. The natural scheme alternates a descent step in $\theta$ and an ascent step in $\phi$,

$$
\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta\,\nabla_\theta\, \ell(\theta_t, \phi_t), \qquad
\phi_{t+1} = \begin{cases} \phi_t + \eta\,\nabla_\phi\, \ell(\theta_{t+1}, \phi_t) & \text{(asynchronous)} \\ \phi_t + \eta\,\nabla_\phi\, \ell(\theta_t, \phi_t) & \text{(synchronous)} \end{cases}
$$

and these naive alternating updates often **fail to converge or even diverge**, circling the solution instead of settling into it. A standard stabilizer is the **extragradient method** (Korpelevich, 1977). It takes a tentative lookahead step, then uses the gradient evaluated at that lookahead point to make the actual update from the original point,

$$
\textbf{Lookahead:} \quad \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta\,\nabla_\theta\, \ell(\theta_t, \phi_t), \qquad \phi_{t+1} = \phi_t + \eta\,\nabla_\phi\, \ell(\theta_t, \phi_t)
$$

$$
\textbf{Update:} \quad \theta_{t+2} = \theta_t - \eta\,\nabla_\theta\, \ell(\theta_{t+1}, \phi_{t+1}), \qquad \phi_{t+2} = \phi_t + \eta\,\nabla_\phi\, \ell(\theta_{t+1}, \phi_{t+1})
$$

The midpoint gradients estimate a better update direction and damp the cycling that plain alternating updates suffer from. This lookahead-then-correct pattern is closely related to **momentum** in ordinary optimizers, in particular Nesterov's accelerated gradient, which likewise evaluates the gradient at an extrapolated point rather than the current one. Here the extrapolation is what lets each player anticipate the opponent's response and steady the updates.

## Wasserstein GANs

Standard GANs work, but training is unstable, mode collapse is common, and the loss curve tells us almost nothing about progress. It is tempting to blame the architecture, but there is a deeper culprit. What if the problem is the **divergence** we are minimizing, the Jensen-Shannon divergence itself?

### Why Jensen-Shannon Fails on Disjoint Supports

Here is a minimal example that exposes the flaw. Let $Z \sim U[0, 1]$ and consider two distributions supported on parallel vertical line segments in the plane,

$$
p = \text{distribution of } (0, Z), \qquad p_\theta = \text{distribution of } (\theta, Z)
$$

{{< figure
    src="/images/ml/ganParallelLines.png"
    caption="The parallel-lines example. The real distribution $p$ places its mass on the vertical line $x = 0$ (blue) and the model $p_\theta$ on the line $x = \theta$ (red). For $\theta \neq 0$ the two supports never overlap."
    alt="Scatter of two parallel vertical lines separated by theta"
>}}

so $p$ sits on the line $x = 0$ and $p_\theta$ on the line $x = \theta$. For any $\theta \neq 0$ the two supports are **disjoint**. Their mixture $m = \tfrac{1}{2}(p + p_\theta)$ equals $\tfrac{1}{2}p$ on the support of $p$ and $\tfrac{1}{2}p_\theta$ on the support of $p_\theta$, so each KL term is

$$
\text{KL}(p \,\|\, m) = \int p(\mathbf{x})\ln\frac{p(\mathbf{x})}{\tfrac{1}{2}p(\mathbf{x})}\, d\mathbf{x} = \ln 2, \qquad \text{KL}(p_\theta \,\|\, m) = \ln 2
$$

and therefore

$$
\text{JS}(p \,\|\, p_\theta) = \frac{1}{2}\ln 2 + \frac{1}{2}\ln 2 = \ln 2 \qquad \text{for every } \theta \neq 0
$$

The Jensen-Shannon divergence is **constant** in $\theta$. It jumps from $0$ at $\theta = 0$ to its maximum $\ln 2$ the instant the lines separate, and stays there. Its gradient is zero almost everywhere, so gradient descent on the generator gets no signal about which direction reduces the gap. This is not a quirk of the example. Whenever the model and data distributions live on low-dimensional manifolds that barely overlap, which is the generic situation in high dimensions, the Jensen-Shannon divergence saturates and the generator's gradient vanishes.

### The Wasserstein Distance

The fix is to measure distance with a divergence that **knows how far apart** the two distributions are, not merely whether they overlap. The **Wasserstein distance** (also called the earth-mover distance) does exactly this. Picture $p$ as a pile of earth and $p_\theta$ as a hole to be filled. A **transport plan** $\gamma(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})$ specifies how much probability mass to move from each point $\mathbf{x}$ in $p$ to each point $\mathbf{y}$ in $p_\theta$. The Wasserstein distance is the cost of the cheapest such plan,

$$
W(p, p_\theta) = \inf_{\gamma \in \Pi(p, p_\theta)} \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) \sim \gamma}\,\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y}\|
$$

where $\Pi(p, p_\theta)$ is the set of joint distributions whose marginals are $p$ and $p_\theta$. The constraint on the marginals is what makes $\gamma$ a valid plan. All the mass of $p$ must be moved, and it must end up distributed as $p_\theta$. The distance is the minimum average distance the mass travels.

On the parallel-lines example the cheapest plan slides each point straight across, a horizontal distance $|\theta|$, giving

$$
W(p, p_\theta) = |\theta|
$$

{{< figure
    src="/images/ml/ganWassersteinParallelLines.png"
    caption="Two transport plans between the parallel lines. A sub-optimal plan (left) moves mass along crossing paths and pays more, while the optimal plan (right) slides every point straight across at horizontal cost $\theta$, so the average distance traveled, and hence the Wasserstein distance, equals $|\theta|$."
    alt="Sub-optimal and optimal transport plans between two parallel lines"
>}}

Compare the two side by side. The Jensen-Shannon divergence is a flat step ($\ln 2$ for $\theta \neq 0$, $0$ at $\theta = 0$) with no usable slope. The Wasserstein distance is the smooth V-shape $|\theta|$, whose gradient points straight at the optimum $\theta = 0$ from anywhere. This is the entire motivation for the Wasserstein GAN. **Wasserstein gives a loss landscape we can actually do gradient descent on, even when the supports are disjoint.**

{{< figure
    src="/images/ml/ganWassersteinGradient.png"
    caption="The Jensen-Shannon divergence (left) is flat at $\ln 2$ for every $\theta \neq 0$, so it gives the generator no gradient. The Wasserstein distance (right) is $|\theta|$, a smooth V whose gradient always points back toward the optimum at $\theta = 0$."
    alt="Jensen-Shannon divergence flat step versus Wasserstein V-shape"
>}}

{{< callout type="info" title="Why Wasserstein is a usable loss" >}}
**Theorem (Arjovsky et al., 2017).** If the generator $G_\theta$ is continuous in $\theta$, then $W(p, p_\theta)$ is continuous in $\theta$. If $G_\theta$ is locally Lipschitz, which any ordinary feedforward network is, then $W(p, p_\theta)$ is continuous everywhere and **differentiable almost everywhere**, so we can compute gradients to optimize it.

Moreover the Wasserstein distance is **weaker** than the Jensen-Shannon divergence in the sense that convergence in JS implies convergence in $W$ but not the other way around. A weaker topology makes it easier for $p_\theta$ to converge to $p$.
{{< /callout >}}

### Kantorovich-Rubinstein Duality

The infimum over all transport plans is intractable, since it searches over the huge space of joint distributions. The **Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality** rewrites the same quantity as a maximization over functions,

$$
W(p, p_\theta) = \sup_{\|f\|_L \le 1}\ \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x} \sim p}[f(\mathbf{x})] - \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x} \sim p_\theta}[f(\mathbf{x})]
$$

Here $\|f\|_L \le 1$ means $f$ is **1-Lipschitz**, $|f(\mathbf{x}) - f(\mathbf{y})| \le \|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y}\|$ for all $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}$. Instead of searching for a transport plan we search for a **potential function** $f$ that is large on real data, small on generated data, but constrained not to change too fast. The harder it is to find such an $f$ achieving a big gap, the closer the two distributions are.

{{< callout type="proof" title="Sketch of Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality" >}}
The primal is a linear program in the plan $\gamma$,

$$
W(p, p_\theta) = \inf_{\gamma \ge 0} \int \gamma(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y}\|\, d\mathbf{x}\, d\mathbf{y} \quad \text{s.t.}\quad \int \gamma(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})\, d\mathbf{y} = p(\mathbf{x}),\ \int \gamma(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})\, d\mathbf{x} = p_\theta(\mathbf{y})
$$

Introduce Lagrange multipliers $f(\mathbf{x})$ and $g(\mathbf{y})$ for the two marginal constraints,

$$
\inf_{\gamma \ge 0}\sup_{f, g} \int \gamma\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y}\|\, d\mathbf{x}\, d\mathbf{y} + \int f(\mathbf{x})\Big(p(\mathbf{x}) - \int \gamma\, d\mathbf{y}\Big)d\mathbf{x} + \int g(\mathbf{y})\Big(p_\theta(\mathbf{y}) - \int \gamma\, d\mathbf{x}\Big)d\mathbf{y}
$$

Grouping the $\gamma$ terms and swapping $\inf$ and $\sup$ by strong duality gives

$$
\sup_{f, g}\ \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x} \sim p}[f(\mathbf{x})] + \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y} \sim p_\theta}[g(\mathbf{y})] + \inf_{\gamma \ge 0}\int \big[\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y}\| - f(\mathbf{x}) - g(\mathbf{y})\big]\gamma(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})\, d\mathbf{x}\, d\mathbf{y}
$$

The inner infimum over $\gamma \ge 0$ is $0$ if the bracket is nonnegative everywhere and $-\infty$ otherwise, so to keep the objective finite we turn it into the constraint $f(\mathbf{x}) + g(\mathbf{y}) \le \|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y}\|$ for all $\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}$. Setting $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}$ forces $g(\mathbf{x}) \le -f(\mathbf{x})$, and the optimum takes $g = -f$. The constraint then reads $f(\mathbf{x}) - f(\mathbf{y}) \le \|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y}\|$, which is exactly that $f$ is 1-Lipschitz. Substituting $g = -f$ recovers

$$
W(p, p_\theta) = \sup_{\|f\|_L \le 1}\ \mathbb{E}_p[f(\mathbf{x})] - \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[f(\mathbf{x})]
$$
{{< /callout >}}

The dual function $f$ replaces the discriminator, and to mark the difference we call it a **critic**. The change of name reflects a change of role. A GAN discriminator is a classifier ending in a sigmoid, it outputs a probability in $[0, 1]$ and answers "is this real?". A WGAN critic has no sigmoid, it outputs an unbounded real-valued score that estimates the dual objective and answers "how much more real-looking is this?". The generator is then trained to push its samples toward higher critic scores, which by the duality is precisely the direction that lowers the Wasserstein distance.

### Enforcing the Lipschitz Constraint

The dual is only valid when $f$ is 1-Lipschitz, so the critic network has to be constrained. Two approaches:

- **Weight clipping** (original WGAN, 2017). After every critic update, clamp each weight into a box,

$$
w \leftarrow \text{clip}(w, -c, c) \qquad (c = 0.01)
$$

If all weights live in a bounded box, the network is $K$-Lipschitz for some $K$. This is crude. The authors themselves called it "a clearly terrible way to enforce a Lipschitz constraint." If $c$ is too large the constraint is loose and training is slow, if $c$ is too small the gradients vanish.

- **Gradient penalty** (WGAN-GP, Gulrajani et al., 2017). A 1-Lipschitz function has gradient norm at most $1$ everywhere, so instead of clipping we add a soft penalty that pushes the critic's gradient norm toward $1$,

$$
\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_p[f(\mathbf{x})] - \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[f(\mathbf{x})] + \lambda\, \mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mathbf{x}} \sim P_{\hat{\mathbf{x}}}}\Big[\big(\|\nabla_{\hat{\mathbf{x}}} f(\hat{\mathbf{x}})\|_2 - 1\big)^2\Big]
$$

The penalty is evaluated at points $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ sampled along the straight lines between real and generated samples, where the constraint matters most. This is the standard modern choice.

### Why This Fixes GAN Training

The Wasserstein formulation **inverts** the central paradox of standard GANs. In a vanilla GAN a too-good discriminator saturates, its gradient flattens, and the generator stops learning, so training becomes a delicate balancing act of keeping the discriminator deliberately weak. In a WGAN, because $W$ is differentiable almost everywhere, the **better** the critic, the **more reliable** the gradient it hands the generator. We therefore want the critic well trained, running roughly $5$ critic steps per generator step so it stays close to optimal, optimized with RMSProp rather than Adam, whose momentum tends to interact badly with the rapidly shifting adversarial objective. The balancing act is gone. Three concrete benefits, each tied to a result above:

1. **No vanishing gradients.** A Lipschitz-constrained critic cannot saturate, so its gradient stays informative everywhere, unlike a classifier that flattens to $0$ or $1$.
2. **Informative loss.** A near-optimal critic's value is an estimate of the Wasserstein distance, so the loss curve actually correlates with sample quality. The vanilla GAN loss does not.
3. **Less mode collapse.** Under Jensen-Shannon, dropping a mode of the data is a local minimum, the missing mode costs nothing because there is no overlap to reward. Under Wasserstein, a dropped mode shows up as transport cost, and the critic exposes that gap as a gradient that pushes the generator outward to cover it.

Today [diffusion models](/garden/ml/computerVision/diffusion) dominate sample quality and training stability, and they have largely taken over high-end image generation. But GANs have not vanished. They remain valuable where fast, single-step sampling matters, since a GAN generates an image in one forward pass while diffusion needs many. The two families have also converged. Adversarial objectives now appear inside diffusion-distillation and consistency-style models, used to make diffusion sampling fast. The lesson is not that GANs lost, but that the field absorbed their ideas.
